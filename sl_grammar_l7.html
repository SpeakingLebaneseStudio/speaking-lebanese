Getting Around & Shopping
Activity 4
Grammar in Context

Four grammar points, one set of errands — the biggest grammar lift in the whole course

Finding your way and buying something both need the same toolkit: words that describe where things are, words that tell someone what to do, the right shape for more than one of something, and a way to say one thing is more than another. This lesson bundles all four — heavier than any single lesson so far, but each piece is small on its own.

You'll lean on vocabulary from this lesson's own bank throughout — wein, janb, leff, mestashfat, r5ees, 8aali — so treat this as practice with words you're already learning, not a fifth new thing to memorize.

Rule 1 — Prepositions of place
noun + preposition + noun
el bank janb el m7attat → the bank is next to the station
No verb needed — Lebanese drops "is/are" here the same way you already learned in Lesson 1's zero-copula pattern.

These prepositions do the heavy lifting for every direction you'll give or get:

المستشفى قدّام الجامع
el mestashfat 2iddém el jémi3
the hospital is in front of the mosque
الصيدلية ورا البنك
el saydalyyet wara el bank
the pharmacy is behind the bank
الدكانة بين الفرن والكنيسة
el dekkénet bein el forn w el kneeset
the shop is between the bakery and the church
el jémi3 — Photo: Ali Marouni / Pexels

wein (where) turns any of these into a question: wein el mestashfat? — the answer just slots a preposition in where "el jémi3" sits above.

Rule 2 — Giving a command (the imperative)
drop the person prefix — add -i for a woman, -o for more than one
leff → turn! (to a man / neutral)
The same -i / -o endings you already know from Lesson 6's btektbi / btektbu — just without the bt- in front.

"leff" (turn!), conjugated for who you're talking to:

leff — "turn!," by who you're speaking to

لَفّ
leff
to a man / neutral
لَفّي
leffi
to a woman
لِفّوا
leffo
to more than one person

Same pattern for the other two imperatives in this lesson's vocab bank — kammel de8ré / kammli de8ré / kammlou de8ré (keep going straight) and w2aaf hon / w2afi hon / w2afo hon (stop here).

Rule 3 — Making a noun plural
two patterns: a regular ending, or an irregular ("broken") change inside the word
m7attat → m7attét (stations)
Regular pattern: drop the final -a/-é, add -et.
كنيسة ← كنايس
kneeset → kanéyes
church → churches (irregular / "broken")
دكانة ← دكاكين
dekkénet → dkékeen
shop → shops (irregular / "broken")
بنك ← بنوك
bank → bnouk
bank → banks (irregular / "broken")
💡
There's no shortcut for "broken" plurals

Unlike the regular -et pattern above, an irregular plural doesn't add a piece to the word — it rearranges the vowels inside it. Kneeset → kanéyes, dekkénet → dkékeen, and bank → bnouk don't share a visible pattern with each other, or with the singular they came from.

✓ m7attat → m7attét — regular, predictable
✓ kneeset → kanéyes — irregular, memorize it

This is completely normal in Lebanese (and Arabic generally) — broken plurals get learned one word at a time, the same way English speakers just know "mouse → mice" isn't "mouse → mouses."

Photo: yl lew / Pexels
Rule 4 — Comparing two things ("more than")
reshape the adjective: aC1C2aC3, then add min (than)
r5ees → ar5as min (cheaper than)
The adjective itself changes shape — no separate word for "more" needed.

This lesson's price words, compared:

هيدا القميص أرخص من هيداك
hayda el 2amees ar5as min haydak
this shirt is cheaper than that one
هيدا الفستان أغلى من هيداك
hayda el fostaan a8la min haydak
this dress is more expensive than that one

hayda / haydak (this one / that one) are already familiar from Lesson 4's demonstratives — comparatives just add a reshaped adjective and min in between.
